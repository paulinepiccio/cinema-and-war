# Cinema and War : What Nations Choose to Film

*An essay based on the analysis of 2,343 war films across six national cinemas (1945-2025).*

---

## Introduction

When a country produces a war film, it does two things at once : it represents a conflict, and it manufactures a memory. *Apocalypse Now* (1979) is not a film about Vietnam, it is a film about America's Vietnam, an American story of a war that America lost. *The Battle of Algiers* (1966), precisely because it is Italian rather than French, says something quite different from what a French film of the same era could have said. Between an event and its film, there is always a choice : a choice to film or to stay silent, a choice to wait or to film urgently, a choice to tell one's own war or someone else's. This essay is interested in that choice, in its patterns and in its dissonances.

The data behind this work consists of 2,343 films produced between 1945 and 2025 across six countries (the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Russia, and Japan) classified across 26 distinct conflicts. The aim was to measure what, behind the apparently global label of "war cinema", actually reflects fragmented and asymmetrical national memories. Numbers alone say little ; but when crossed with the geopolitical context of each country, they map out a singular geography of remembrance and forgetting. 

*The full methodology used to assemble this corpus is laid out at the end of this document.*

Three questions structure what follows. First : is there one war cinema, or several ? Beyond a common reference point (World War II) national volumes and rhythms diverge so sharply that the unity of the genre becomes questionable. Acknowledging that plurality leads to a second question : what does each national cinema choose to represent, and just as importantly, what does it choose to leave out ? And then a third, perhaps the most revealing of all: when does a country film its wars ? Some conflicts are filmed almost in real time ; others demand a generation of silence before they can become images. Taken together, these three questions sketch a war cinema that is less universal than it appears. 

---

## I. One war cinema, or several ?

The first observation, which may seem obvious, is worth setting out for what it later reveals. At first glance, all six countries produce war films, and all six film the same founding conflict in great quantity : World War II. Viewed from a distance, the unity of the genre seems established.

<img width="801" height="452" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/b70c2f6e-0868-4426-a6fa-f599c43b0370" />

Yet the moment one looks at production volumes more closely, the asymmetry is striking. The United States alone accounts for nearly half the classified corpus (44.9%), followed at a distance by the United Kingdom (17.6%). Russia (14.4%), France (11.6%), Germany (7.3%), and Japan (4.3%) trail behind in that order. Hollywood is not merely *a* war cinema among others ; statistically, it is *the* world's war cinema. The sheer gap between the United States and Japan (more than ten times as many films on the American side) already suggests that what we call "war cinema" does not designate a unified reality, but a deeply uneven production landscape. This volume reflects both the size of national film industries and the anglo-centric coverage of the TMDB database, which structurally under-represents non-English-language cinemas.

The diagnosis becomes clearer when one looks at the temporal rhythm of production. Five of the six countries reach their historical peak in the 2010s, in what appears to be a contemporary boom for the genre (driven by streaming platforms, anniversary commemorations (1945, Vietnam), and ongoing conflicts (Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, Ukraine). All five, except Russia.

<img width="1342" height="537" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/142b0df5-f68d-45f9-8784-c3c9e297d486" />

Russia is the only country whose peak is not in the 2010s, but in the 1960s (21.6%), followed by a second peak in the 1970s (17.3%). This is no detail. The double peak corresponds to the golden age of Soviet cinema on the "Great Patriotic War": Tarkovsky and *Ivan's Childhood* (1962), Kalatozov and *The Cranes Are Flying* (1957). It is also the era when, under Khrushchev and then Brezhnev, the memory of 1945 became the ideological cement of a post-Stalinist Soviet regime in search of renewed legitimacy. The deep collapse of the 1990s (5.3%) tells, in reverse, the story of a Russian film industry disintegrating after the fall of the USSR, before a post-2000 revival largely sustained by Putin-era state funding. This unique temporal profile, shared by no other country, is by itself enough to undermine the idea of a unified global war cinema.

France, in its own way, also shows an atypical curve : a remarkable U-shape, with very low output in the immediate postwar years (4.2 to 4.8% in the 1940s-50s, the lowest share of any Western country during that period), a jump in the 1960s (15.6%), and a steady rise to the 2010s peak (22.2%). This relative postwar silence does not mean France stopped filming the war altogether : *La Bataille du rail* (1946) and *Le Silence de la mer* (1949) prove otherwise from the immediate aftermath. What these films mostly represented, however, was a resistant France, in keeping with the national myth constructed by de Gaulle after 1944. It would take *Marcel Ophüls's The Sorrow and the Pity* (1969), then Louis Malle's *Lacombe Lucien* (1974), for French cinema to truly start filming the grey zones : collaboration, state antisemitism, the ambiguities of Vichy. The relative silence of the 1940s-50s was therefore not an absence of cinema, but a selective cinema, one that filmed part of the war while avoiding the rest.

If we can speak of "war cinema" in the singular, it is only in the sense that several national cinemas coexist under a common label. The statistical reality suggests something rather different : there are several war cinemas, profoundly distinct in their volumes, their rhythms, and their historical centers of gravity.

## II. What does it mean to film a war ?

Once the plurality of the genre is acknowledged, the question becomes: what distinguishes one war cinema from another? If each country films war in its own way, it is first because each country films different wars. The cross-mapping of countries and conflicts reveals what one might call **national signatures**.

<img width="1947" height="2376" alt="03_heatmap_conflict_country (6)" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8ecc2245-bec9-4fe2-be04-cd6f3b1ede8c" />

This heatmap reveals an almost mechanical diagonal of appropriations. World War II dominates everywhere, but unequally: from 65.9% in the United States to 95.2% in Japan, a thirty-point gap. The Japanese figure alone deserves attention. That Japan dedicates 95% of its war cinema to a single conflict is not a cultural enigma; it is the direct consequence of Article 9 of its 1947 Constitution, by which the country "forever renounces war as a sovereign right of the nation." To film a war, one must first have one to film. Japanese cinema, deprived since 1947 of significant military engagements, returns to the tragic conflict of 1939-1945, seen from Hiroshima, Nagasaki, the firebombing of Tokyo, and the imperial memory that was never fully reckoned with.

At the other end of the spectrum, the United States shows the most diversified profile : 65.9% on World War II, certainly, but also 14% on Vietnam, 5.9% on Afghanistan, 4.2% on Korea, 3.5% on Iraq. Hollywood covers roughly one war per decade since 1945, as American military engagements have continually renewed themselves. The American signature isn't a particular conflict, it's an appetite : that of a superpower which, rather than having a subject, produces them in series.

Between these two extremes, each country develops its own signature. France dedicates 5.4% of its war cinema to the Algerian War, a conflict that no other country films in any significant way (0.2% in the United States, 0% elsewhere). Russia has made the Chechen Wars its near-exclusive domain (3.8% on the First Chechen War, against 0% everywhere else). Germany, more surprisingly, devotes 5.7% of its war cinema to the Cold War, more than triple the American share (1.7%). The statistical anomaly evaporates the moment one remembers that Germany was, for four decades, the only country in the world to have been literally cut in two by the Cold War. German cinema that addresses the Cold War doesn't view it as an abstract geopolitical event, the way Hollywood handles espionage ; it films it from inside the country, from the kitchen, the bedroom, the surveilled writer's study. *The Lives of Others* (2006), which won Florian Henckel von Donnersmarck an Academy Award for Best Foreign Film, *Good Bye, Lenin!* (2003), and *The Tunnel* (2001) form a distinctly German subgenre, with an intimate register no other cinema could produce.

The United Kingdom shows the most dispersed profile of the corpus. Its signature isn't a conflict but a scatter : the Falklands (1.6%), Northern Ireland (1.2%), Kosovo, Malaya, the Hungarian Revolution, small marks on the heatmap that together sketch, in negative, the geography of a defeated empire. British cinema seems to revisit by fragments the edges of what was once its territory ; the confetti of an empire continues to produce images long after the empire itself has ceased to exist.

But once these signatures are identified, a more subtle question arises: does each country actually film its own wars, or also those of others? Here a second analysis, on the nature of narration, reveals an unexpected feature.

<img width="1189" height="590" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/c37fb5fb-ea67-4107-9b82-a36750c12c0b" />
<img width="1191" height="589" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/b85537aa-93cd-4671-8e58-60243d541100" />

Once World War II is removed (it mechanically involves all six countries and skews the reading) three groups emerge. The United States (95.9%) and France (93.9%) are fiercely self-focused cinema : they film their own wars and almost exclusively their own. The United Kingdom (75.6% to 24.4%) and Russia (66.7% to 33.3%) display a mixed profile. The UK films a substantial share of foreign conflicts, but with a striking peculiarity: of ten British films covering foreign conflicts, six concern the Vietnam War, an American war in which Britain did not participate. The Anglo-American cultural ecosystem is at work, making British cinema a constant cultural partner of the Hollywood narrative.

The Russian case is more singular still. Of the seven Russian films covering conflicts in which Russia was not involved, **all seven** treat the post-2001 War in Afghanistan, the American war. The coincidence is anything but. The Soviet Union lost its own Afghan war (1979-1989), and contemporary Russian cinema, by filming the American war in the same theater, performs something one might call a retro-projection: commenting on someone else's defeat in order to avoid speaking of one's own, or perhaps, more subtly, speaking of one's own defeat by speaking of someone else's.

The two outliers remain. Germany, with its 50/50 split, is the most outward-looking cinema in the corpus, dividing its non-WWII output evenly between its own engagements (Bosnia, Kosovo, NATO operations in Afghanistan) and foreign conflicts (Vietnam, Iraq, the Gulf War, Syria). Japan, in inverse asymmetry, shows the opposite : its three non-WWII films deal entirely (100%) with foreign conflicts (Vietnam, Korea, Bosnia). Constitutionally pacifist Japan, when it does film a war outside its own, does so as a pure observer, never as a participant. The statistical fragility of this last observation should be noted (three films) but the overall pattern is consistent.

## III. When does a country film its wars ?

To close this inquiry, one last dimension remains, the one that secretly structures all the others : time. Is a war filmed while the fighting still rages, immediately after, or several decades later, once one generation has handed the watching over to the next ?

<img width="1292" height="590" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/3db542d0-930d-49e3-a69e-7d8348f05c90" />

The overall answer is clear-cut : 74.5% of films are released more than ten years after the end of the conflict they depict. War cinema is, first and foremost, a cinema of memory. Only 10.9% of films are released during the conflict itself, and 14.6% in the decade that follows. For war to become film, time is needed, often a great deal of it. But this average masks considerable disparities, which extend and confirm what the national signatures had already begun to suggest.

The United States, true to its appetite, are the most contemporary of their wars : 34% of American films are released within ten years of a conflict's end, making Hollywood the only cinema that truly films in the heat of things. It is, in a sense, the near-real-time chronicler of American military engagements : *American Sniper* (2014) was released three years after the official end of the Iraq War, while *Lone Survivor* (2013) and *12 Strong* (2018) addressed the Afghan war while American troops were still on the ground. At the opposite extreme, Japan (95.2% long-term memory) and Russia (84.1%) cultivate a fundamentally retrospective relationship with war. Japan has no choice, given the absence of recent conflicts ; Russia, more paradoxically, chooses it, piling up films on 1945 while contemporary Russian conflicts remain largely under-treated on screen.

But the temporal distribution becomes most revealing when one looks at conflicts rather than countries.

<img width="1292" height="790" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/57340bd4-9a3d-42f4-a831-b8dac43f0244" />

Three categories emerge. "Hot" conflicts are filmed mostly while they are happening : the post-2001 War in Afghanistan (83.3%), the Cold War (54.2%), which lasted long enough to become a cinematic genre within itself, through John Le Carré-style espionage and Ian Fleming adaptations, and the Iraq War (51.6%). "Warm" conflicts are filmed mostly in the decade following their end : the Gulf War (80% aftermath), the Korean War (63.3%), the Bosnian War (53.8%). "Cold" conflicts, finally, require more than ten years before being filmed in any significant volume : World War II (87.4% long-term), the Algerian War (80%), the Vietnam War (61.7%).

It is not insignificant that the three slowest conflicts to become cinema are also the most morally complex. World War II raised questions of collaboration, genocide, and atomic bombing. Algeria raised those of colonialism. Vietnam, those of a superpower's moral defeat. The Gulf War (1990-91) became filmable quickly because it was quickly won and politically uncontroversial ; the Algerian War didn't, because it confronts France with a question France has long preferred not to ask.

This last observation leads to a final question, perhaps the most instructive of all : what is filmed, and what is not ?

<img width="2592" height="1968" alt="06_universality_ranking (1)" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/b0322b84-ba81-4d3a-9c69-673727a53554" />

Of the twenty-six conflicts classified, only five (19%) are filmed by five or six countries, and even the "universality" of those five is very relative. The Vietnam War is filmed 85% by the Americans ; the War in Afghanistan, 63% ; the Cold War, 46%. The only genuinely balanced universality concerns World War II, where the six countries contribute in roughly comparable proportions (United States 39%, others between 7% and 19%). For the rest, what we take for the universal turns out to be Hollywood hegemony with foreign contributions in supporting roles.

More telling still : six conflicts in the corpus generated only **one film each**. The Mau Mau Uprising in Kenya (1952-1960), the Hungarian Revolution of 1956, the Malayan Emergency (1948-1960), the Libyan Civil War, the Kosovo War, the Yom Kippur War. These are not minor conflicts : they sometimes mobilized tens of thousands of combatants, redrew borders, destabilized entire regions. But none of the six national cinemas studied has truly taken hold of them. More striking still, the single film on the Mau Mau Uprising was produced not by the United Kingdom, the colonial power directly involved, but by the United States. British cinema falls silent on one of its own most violent colonial chapters. This silence, statistically aberrant, is historically eloquen t: it belongs to a long British tradition of holding imperial memory at arm's length.

These cinematic silences are perhaps more telling than anything else in this study: they trace, in negative, the part of the world that cinema cannot or will not look at.

## Conclusion

By the end of this inquiry, war cinema appears less as a global genre than as a mosaic of national memories, each obeying its own temporality, its own obsessions, its own silences. To the first question, that of the genre's unity, the data answers with dispersion : there are not one but six war cinemas, profoundly different in their volumes and rhythms. To the second, that of what is represented, the data answers with national signatures : France and Algeria, Germany and the Cold War, Russia and Chechnya, the United Kingdom and the edges of its empire, Japan and the only war it ever lost. To the third, that of time, the data answers with temporal asymmetry : some wars are filmed hot, others demand a generation of silence before they can become images at all.

What the numbers ultimately reveal, *in fine*, is less what happened than what each country decided to keep (and to omit) on its screens. War cinema is, in this sense, a politics of memory before it is an aesthetic. To the historian belongs the task of establishing the facts ; to cinema, a more ambiguous but perhaps more powerful task: deciding which of those facts deserve to keep being watched.

## Methodology

The corpus of 2,343 films analyzed here was assembled using the API of The Movie Database (TMDB), queried for all films classified under the "war" genre since 1939, across the six target countries. The 1945-2025 period was then retained for analysis, with one exception : World War II (1939-1945) was kept, since omitting it would have artificially excluded the foundational conflict of twentieth-century war cinema.

One methodological detail is worth flagging. A first version of the corpus showed a striking anomaly : zero classified Russian films before 1990. Investigation revealed that TMDB indexes Soviet-era films under the country code "SU" (Soviet Union), distinct from "RU" (Russia). The initial collection, querying only "RU", had therefore missed nearly half a century of one of the richest war cinemas in the world. A second version of the pipeline re-integrated "SU" and merged the two sets, recovering 136 additional classified films and making possible the comparison with the golden age of Soviet cinema. The lesson is worth remembering : when a dataset shows such a clear-cut statistical gap, the first suspect should be the pipeline itself, not the phenomenon being observed.

After collection, the data was cleaned by deduplicating co-productions and applying an adaptive filtering by country, designed to compensate as much as possible for the anglo-centric bias of the TMDB database, a bias that, without correction, would have over-filtered non-English-language cinemas through audience effect.

The most delicate stage was classifying films by conflict. Two successive layers were used. The first queried film synopses with a dictionary of keywords built conflict by conflict, with particular attention to word boundaries, to avoid, for instance, the keyword "ira" being triggered inside "iraqi". The second layer, for films not classified by the first pass, queried TMDB's community-curated keywords API, which provides for each film a list of thematic tags maintained by platform users. All the films showing obvious temporal inconsistencies (films classified to a war ending decades before their release) were manually re-classified. At the end of the process, 1,445 films out of 2,185 "in-scope" films were attributed to a conflict, for an effective matching rate of 66.1%. A stratified manual audit of 30 films estimated the classification accuracy at around 80-85%.

---

## Limits of the analysis

Three limits should be kept in mind when reading this inquiry. The corpus relies on the TMDB database, whose structurally anglo-centric coverage under-represents non-English-language cinemas, particularly those of Asia and the former Soviet bloc before the 1990s. The adaptive filtering mentioned above partially compensates for this bias without correcting it fully. The automated classification of films, in turn, reaches an estimated accuracy of 80-85%, meaning that roughly one film in six may be incorrectly attributed to a conflict ; this margin of error does not invalidate the broader trends identified, but invites them to be read for what they are : orders of magnitude rather than definitive figures. Finally, a part of global cinematic memory, the part not indexed by TMDB, the part not internationally distributed, necessarily escapes this study.
